2.3 Satellite navigation (GNSS)

How it works

Each navigation satellite broadcasts a signal with its precise position and the time from an atomic clock. A receiver measures how long each signal took to arrive and converts it into a distance (a "pseudorange"). With distances to several satellites it computes its position by trilateration.

  • You need at least four satellites: three unknowns for position (x, y, z) plus one for the error of the receiver's cheap clock.
  • Positioning, Navigation and Timing (PNT): timing is just as important as position. Telecom networks, power grids and financial markets synchronise their clocks with GNSS.
  • Main error sources: ionosphere and troposphere delays, satellite clock and orbit errors, multipath (reflections off buildings), poor geometry, interference.

Improving accuracy and trust

TechniqueHow it worksTypical accuracy
Standalone, single frequencyBasic smartphone positioninga few metres
Dual frequencyRemoves most of the ionospheric error (Galileo E1 + E5)better than standalone, under a few metres
SBAS (e.g. EGNOS)Geostationary satellites broadcast corrections and integrity information over a region1–2 m, with guaranteed integrity for aviation
PPP (e.g. Galileo HAS)Precise orbit and clock corrections from satellites; needs time to convergedecimetres (≈20 cm for HAS)
RTKCorrections from a nearby reference stationcentimetres

Integrity is the ability to warn the user when the position should not be trusted. It matters for safety-of-life uses such as aviation.

Threats: jamming and spoofing

  • Jamming: transmitting noise so the receiver loses the very weak GNSS signal. Widespread interference has affected aviation in the Baltic region and the Middle East.
  • Spoofing: transmitting fake signals so the receiver computes a wrong position or time without noticing. More dangerous because it is silent.
  • Countermeasures: authentication (Galileo OSNMA), encrypted signals (PRS), multi-constellation receivers, antennas that reject interference, alternative PNT (LEO-PNT, terrestrial systems, inertial sensors).

Galileo

The EU's global navigation system, under civilian control. Satellites in medium Earth orbit (about 23,200 km). A second generation (G2) is in development.

EGNOS

European SBAS. It monitors GPS (and in future Galileo, with EGNOS V3) and broadcasts corrections and integrity over Europe through geostationary satellites. Main use: aircraft approaches (LPV procedures) without ground equipment at the airport; also agriculture and maritime.

Why Galileo if GPS is free?

  1. Strategic autonomy: GPS belongs to the US military; access could be degraded in a crisis.
  2. Performance: with GPS + Galileo (+ others) receivers see more satellites, so accuracy and availability improve, especially in "urban canyons".
  3. Distinctive services: HAS, OSNMA, SAR return link, PRS.
  4. Industrial and economic: European industry and jobs, and a seat at the table in international standards.

The honest economic point

Most receivers are multi-constellation. The economic value of Galileo is therefore its marginal value over GPS and the others, not the value of positioning as a whole. Raise this yourself: it shows you understand CBA (see section 3.6).

LEO-PNT

Positioning, navigation and timing from low Earth orbit (roughly 500–1,200 km) instead of medium orbit.

  • Advantages: signals arrive much stronger (shorter distance), so they resist jamming better; satellites move fast across the sky, so precise solutions converge faster; possible new frequencies.
  • Disadvantages: many more satellites are needed for global coverage; shorter satellite life; very precise orbit and clock determination is needed.
  • Role: a complement to Galileo for resilience and performance, not a replacement.
  • ESA Celeste: in-orbit demonstrator. The first two satellites were launched at the end of March 2026 on a Rocket Lab Electron; about eight more are planned from 2027.
  • Commercial players are also developing LEO-PNT services. In any EU CBA they belong in the options or the counterfactual.
"Galileo gives Europe autonomy and services GPS doesn't offer, like authentication and free high accuracy. LEO-PNT would add resilience against jamming and spoofing, which is now a real operational problem, but the CBA has to compare it with what Galileo and commercial providers already deliver."

Most of the economic value is created at the right end of the chain. Free data are necessary but not sufficient: users need processed, ready-to-use information.

Sentinel vs commercial very-high-resolution data

Copernicus also buys data from contributing missions, often commercial satellites with sub-metre resolution. New Space constellations now offer very high resolution with daily or even sub-daily revisit.

When is paying worth it? Only when the extra resolution or revisit changes a decision: small objects (vehicles, buildings), urban change, infrastructure inspection, insurance claims, emergencies where hours matter. For crop monitoring at field level, Sentinel-2 at 10 m is often enough. This is a value-of-information question (section 3.5).

"Copernicus data are free and open, but uptake depends on turning data into ready-to-use services. Commercial high-resolution data add value only where they change a decision, so I would assess them with a value-of-information approach."

GOVSATCOM

Pooling and sharing of existing secure satellite capacity (national military and commercial) for EU and Member State governmental users: crisis management, surveillance, key infrastructure. A component of the 2021–2027 programme, with ground "hubs" connecting users to capacity.

IRIS²

Infrastructure for Resilience, Interconnectivity and Security by Satellite. An EU-owned multi-orbit constellation (LEO and MEO) for secure governmental communications, with commercial services on the same infrastructure.

  • Delivery model: 12-year concession to the SpaceRISE consortium (SES, Eutelsat, Hispasat), signed on 16 December 2024. A public-private partnership: the EU and ESA finance part, the private partners finance and operate the rest and sell commercial services.
  • Initial plan: about 290 satellites, about €10.6bn in total, of which about €6.5bn public; governmental services targeted for 2030.
  • [check] In 2026 the EU and SpaceRISE announced a reinforcement (more satellites, more governmental capacity) and an acceleration to 2029. Press figures on satellites and budget vary.
  • Rationale: sovereignty, security of communications, reducing dependence on non-EU systems such as Starlink (highlighted by the war in Ukraine), connectivity in remote areas, quantum-encryption readiness.

EUSPA's 2026 market report estimates EU demand for secure satcom data services growing from just over €0.2bn (2025) to about €1.2bn (2040), led today by maritime surveillance and later by law enforcement, civil protection and force deployment.

2.6 Space situational awareness (SSA)

  • Space Surveillance and Tracking (EU SST): a partnership of Member States providing collision-avoidance warnings, re-entry and fragmentation analysis.
  • Space weather: solar activity that can disrupt GNSS, satellites and power grids.
  • Near-Earth objects: asteroids and comets.

The issue is growing fast with mega-constellations and debris: the EU Space Act (below) addresses it through regulation.

2.7 Policies and documents to know

EU Space Act (proposal)

  • Commission proposal COM(2025) 335, 25 June 2025: a regulation on the safety, resilience and sustainability of space activities.
  • Safety: tracking, collision avoidance, debris mitigation, end-of-life rules.
  • Resilience: cybersecurity and risk management for space operators, as a lex specialis to the NIS2 Directive.
  • Sustainability: a common method to measure the environmental footprint of space activities.
  • Applies to EU and non-EU operators serving the EU market; expected to apply from 2030. [check] Status in Parliament (ITRE committee) and Council.
  • For a consultancy: impact assessments, compliance-cost studies, support to implementation.

European Court of Auditors, Special Report 07/2021

"EU space programmes Galileo and Copernicus: services launched, but the uptake needs a further boost".

  • Copernicus data used mainly by experts and scientists; Commission actions had not produced the hoped-for rise in uptake.
  • Weak monitoring of Galileo uptake; some key features not yet available at the time.
  • No comprehensive strategy with measurable, time-bound objectives.
  • Recommendations: a comprehensive uptake strategy; a framework to assess economic and societal benefits; integrated access to Copernicus data; identification of regulatory and administrative barriers.
  • Follow-up: in 2024 the Commission published a staff working document on uptake with a conceptual framework to measure it.

Supporting EUSPA in implementing these recommendations is a typical assignment: you should be able to propose KPIs (section 4.4).

Other references

  • EU Space Strategy for Security and Defence (March 2023): space as a security domain, protection of space assets, resilience.
  • European Chips Act (2023): relevant to the project on semiconductors for defence and space.
  • EUSPA EU Space Market Report (May 2026): the reference for downstream market figures.

3. Cost-benefit analysis

Cost-benefit analysis (CBA) measures whether an intervention increases the welfare of society. It is not about whether the project is profitable for the operator; it is about whether society is better off with it than without it. Reference: the European Commission's Guide to Cost-Benefit Analysis of Investment Projects (2014) and the Better Regulation Toolbox.

3.1 Time value of money

€100 today is worth more than €100 in ten years: money can be invested, people prefer consumption now, and the future is uncertain. To compare flows at different dates we discount them to their present value.

PV = X / (1 + r)t    Discount factor DFt = 1 / (1 + r)t
Annuity (constant X for years 1…n): PV = X × [1 − (1 + r)−n] / r

Example: €100m received in year 10 at 3% is worth 100 / 1.0310 = 100 / 1.344 = €74.4m today. Discounting is compound, not linear: 100 × (1 − 0.03 × 10) = €70m is wrong.

Real vs nominal

  • Constant prices (inflation removed) → use a real discount rate.
  • Current prices (including inflation) → use a nominal rate.
  • Link: (1 + nominal) = (1 + real) × (1 + inflation). Never mix the two.

Which discount rate?

  • Social discount rate (economic analysis): the 2014 Commission Guide uses a benchmark of 3% real, and 5% for cohesion countries; the Better Regulation Toolbox recommends 3%.
  • Financial discount rate: benchmark of 4% real in the 2014 Guide.
  • A higher rate penalises projects whose benefits come late. Space infrastructure has high upfront costs and long benefit streams, so results are sensitive to the rate.

Step 1 – Context and objectives

What problem does the intervention address? What are the objectives, ideally quantified? Example: "reduce the economic exposure of critical infrastructure to GNSS disruption".

Step 2 – Counterfactual and options

The counterfactual (or baseline) is what happens without the intervention. Everything in a CBA is measured as the difference between the scenario with the project and the counterfactual: the incremental approach.

  • Do-nothing: no new action; existing assets keep running.
  • Do-minimum: only what is needed to keep the current service level.
  • Options: realistic alternatives (e.g. build an EU system, buy a commercial service, upgrade existing systems). A CBA of only one option against do-nothing is weak.

Typical mistake

Choosing a counterfactual that is too pessimistic makes any project look good. In space, the counterfactual must include what Galileo, GPS and commercial providers will deliver anyway.

Step 3 – Financial analysis

From the point of view of the owner or operator: investment, operating costs, revenues, residual value. Indicators: financial net present value (FNPV) and financial rate of return (FRR). It shows if the project needs public funding and if it is financially sustainable (cash never runs out). Public space infrastructure usually has a negative FNPV: that is why it is public.

Step 4 – Economic analysis

From the point of view of society. Starting from the financial flows:

  1. Remove transfers: taxes, subsidies and other payments between parts of society do not create or destroy resources.
  2. Apply shadow prices (conversion factors) where market prices are distorted, e.g. labour in areas of high unemployment.
  3. Add externalities and non-market benefits: time saved, lives saved, emissions avoided, damage avoided.
  4. Discount at the social discount rate.
ENPV = Σt (Bt − Ct) / (1 + r)t
B/C ratio = PV(benefits) / PV(costs)
ERR = the discount rate at which ENPV = 0
IndicatorRuleStrengths and limits
ENPV> 0The main indicator: absolute net gain for society. Depends on project size.
B/C ratio> 1Easy to communicate; independent of size. Sensitive to whether a cost is netted from benefits or added to costs.
ERR (IRR)> social discount rateIntuitive. Can be misleading: multiple IRRs if flows change sign several times; does not rank projects of different size well.

Other elements

  • Time horizon: the economic life of the asset (for satellites, typically 10–15 years per generation; for systems, longer with replenishment).
  • Residual value: value of assets at the end of the horizon, if any.
  • Sunk costs: costs already spent are excluded; only future, avoidable costs count.
  • Replacement and replenishment: satellites must be replaced; forgetting this is a classic error.

3.4 Risk and sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analysis

Change one variable at a time and see how the result moves. The 2014 Guide defines a variable as critical when a 1% change in it causes a change of more than 1% in the NPV.

Sensitivity of the worked example
ScenarioENPV (€m)B/C
Base case+2.501.10

Benefits −20%−2.960.88

All variables are critical here: a 1% fall in benefits reduces ENPV by €0.27m, about 11% of €2.50m.

Switching values

The switching value is the percentage change in a variable that brings the NPV to zero.

Switching value of benefits = ENPV / PV(benefits) = 2.50 / 27.30 = 9.2%
Switching value of operating costs = ENPV / PV(opex) = 2.50 / 12.80 = 19.5%
Switching value of investment = ENPV / investment = 2.50 / 12.0 = 20.8%

Interpretation: if avoided damage is overestimated by just 9.2%, the project no longer pays off. Given how uncertain flood damage estimates are, the right message to the client is "positive but fragile; the result depends on the benefit estimate, which needs better evidence".

Scenario and probabilistic analysis

  • Scenario analysis: change several variables together in consistent stories (pessimistic, base, optimistic).
  • Monte Carlo: give each critical variable a probability distribution, draw thousands of combinations, and report the distribution of NPV, e.g. "probability that ENPV < 0 is 35%".
  • Qualitative risk assessment: risk matrix (probability × impact) with mitigation measures, e.g. launch failure, delays, low user adoption.
"I'd always report switching values, because a positive NPV that turns negative with a 9% error in benefits is a very different message from one that survives a 50% error."

Careful with industry effects

In a CBA, jobs and revenues of the space industry are mostly not benefits: the money spent on the project is a cost, and the workers would otherwise be employed elsewhere. They belong in an economic impact study (section 3.8), not in the ENPV. Mixing the two is a common error.

Value of information: worked example

A farm cooperative manages 1,000 ha. A disease hits about 20% of fields each season, but nobody knows which ones. Treatment costs €100/ha; an untreated infected field loses €600/ha.

  • Without information: treat everything (1,000 × €100 = €100,000) or treat nothing (200 ha × €600 = €120,000 losses). Best choice: treat everything, cost €100,000.
  • With perfect satellite information: treat only the 200 infected hectares, cost 200 × €100 = €20,000.
  • Value of information = €100,000 − €20,000 = €80,000 per season. If the data and analytics cost €5/ha (€5,000), the net value is €75,000.
  • With imperfect detection (missed or false alarms) the value falls: a realistic estimate needs the detection accuracy.

Key lesson: data have value only if they change a decision. If the farmer would act the same way anyway, the value is zero, whatever the resolution.

3.6 Attribution and additionality

Not every benefit observed after an intervention is caused by it. Three concepts:

  • Deadweight: what would have happened anyway (in the counterfactual).
  • Displacement: benefits that are simply moved from one place or company to another.
  • Attribution: the share of a benefit that is due to this intervention when several factors contribute.

The GNSS attribution problem

Modern receivers combine GPS, Galileo, GLONASS and BeiDou. The benefit of positioning in a segment is not the benefit of Galileo. A standard approach:

Benefit attributable to Galileo = Total benefit in the segment
  × share of devices that are Galileo-enabled
  × share of the benefit that depends on Galileo specifically

Example: precision agriculture generates €500m of annual benefits; 80% of receivers are Galileo-enabled; Galileo's specific contribution (more satellites, HAS, authentication, continuity if GPS degrades) is estimated at 20% → 500 × 0.8 × 0.2 = €80m, not €500m.

The last factor is the most uncertain: estimate it with expert interviews, receiver tests and scenarios (e.g. "GPS unavailable for 24 hours"), and test it in the sensitivity analysis.

3.7 Cost-effectiveness and multi-criteria analysis

Cost-effectiveness analysis (CEA)

When benefits cannot be monetised but the objective is fixed, compare options by cost per unit of result: e.g. cost per hour of guaranteed timing availability, or the cheapest way to reach a required resilience level.

Multi-criteria analysis (MCA)

Used for strategic choices with several non-monetary dimensions (autonomy, security, time to service). Steps: define criteria; give weights; score each option; compute weighted scores; test how results change with the weights.

Illustrative MCA: options for resilient PNT (scores 1–5, higher is better)
CriterionWeight AWeight BEU-owned LEO-PNTBuy commercial serviceDo-minimum

Lesson: with weights A the ranking is do-minimum first; with weights B (more weight on resilience, less on cost) the EU-owned option wins. MCA results depend on the weights, so the weights must be justified (e.g. by stakeholder consultation) and their sensitivity shown.

3.8 Economic impact studies

Different from CBA. They answer "how much economic activity does the space sector generate?", not "is it worth it?".

  • Direct impact: turnover, gross value added (GVA) and jobs of space companies.
  • Indirect: activity in the supply chain.
  • Induced: activity from the spending of wages.
  • Usually estimated with input-output multipliers (Type I = direct + indirect; Type II = + induced).
  • Use GVA, not turnover, to avoid double counting along the chain.
"An impact study tells you how big the activity is; a CBA tells you whether society is better off. I'd keep jobs and industrial turnover out of the NPV and report them separately."

4. Evaluation and impact assessment

The Commission's Better Regulation framework (guidelines and toolbox) sets how EU interventions are designed, assessed and evaluated. Much of the team's public work fits into it.

4.1 The intervention logic

→

→

Example for Copernicus uptake actions: activities = training and user workshops; outputs = number of people trained; results = new users actively using Copernicus data; impacts = better environmental decisions, new businesses, jobs.

External factors also influence results and impacts: the evaluation must separate the effect of the intervention from them.

4.2 Evaluation (ex post)

Asks whether an existing intervention works. Five Better Regulation criteria:

CriterionQuestionExample for Copernicus
EffectivenessDid it achieve its objectives?Has the number of non-expert users grown as planned?
EfficiencyAt what cost? Were resources used well?Cost per active user; administrative burden of data access
RelevanceDo the objectives still match the needs?Do services match what agriculture and insurance need now?
CoherenceDoes it fit with other interventions (internal and external)?Overlap or synergy with national EO programmes and Horizon Europe
EU added valueWhat does EU action add compared with Member States alone?Economies of scale, common data policy, single access

Trap

The OECD-DAC criteria used in development cooperation are relevance, coherence, effectiveness, efficiency, impact and sustainability. The EU Better Regulation list has EU added value instead. Don't mix them.

Evaluation methods

  • Evaluation matrix: for each evaluation question, judgement criteria, indicators, data sources.
  • Data: monitoring data, desk research, surveys, interviews, case studies, focus groups, workshops.
  • Triangulation: answer each question with at least two independent sources.
  • Counterfactual methods (comparison groups, difference-in-differences) where data allow; otherwise contribution analysis: build a plausible chain of evidence that the intervention contributed to the observed change.

4.3 Impact assessment (ex ante)

Prepared before the Commission proposes a significant initiative (e.g. the EU Space Act). Standard structure:

  1. Problem definition: what is the problem, its drivers, who is affected, how it evolves without action.
  2. Why should the EU act? Legal basis, subsidiarity (can Member States do it alone?) and EU added value.
  3. Objectives: general, specific, operational; SMART (Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound).
  4. Policy options, always including the baseline.
  5. Impacts: economic, social, environmental; on SMEs, competitiveness, fundamental rights; administrative burden (the Standard Cost Model counts time × frequency × tariff for information obligations). The Commission applies a "one in, one out" approach to new burdens.
  6. Comparison of options: effectiveness, efficiency, coherence, proportionality.
  7. Monitoring and evaluation arrangements.

Quality control: the Regulatory Scrutiny Board. Stakeholders are consulted, typically through a public consultation of 12 weeks.

4.4 KPIs and monitoring frameworks

Essential for the ECA follow-up project. Good KPIs are linked to the intervention logic and have a baseline, a target, a source and a frequency.

LevelWeak KPIBetter KPI
OutputNumber of events organisedNumber of users trained who report using Copernicus data within 6 months
ResultNumber of downloadsNumber of active users by segment; share of new GNSS devices that are Galileo-enabled; share using HAS or OSNMA
Impact"Increased awareness"Revenues of EU downstream companies; estimated socio-economic benefits per segment
"I'd build KPIs from the intervention logic, with a baseline and target for each, and favour result indicators like active users and Galileo-enabled market share over activity counts like events or downloads."

4.5 Gap analysis and user requirements

  • Gap analysis: current state vs desired state → size of each gap → causes (barriers) → actions to close it → priority.
  • User needs and requirements: what users need (accuracy, availability, integrity, latency, revisit, price, formats, certification) translated into requirements for the system. EUSPA publishes User Requirements Reports by segment and runs a User Consultation Platform.
  • Distinguish needs (the problem to solve) from requirements (measurable performance specifications).

5. Market analysis and forecasting

5.1 Defining the market

Before any number, define: product or service, geography (EU, global), revenue type (devices, services, data, value added), year and currency. Most disagreements between market studies come from different definitions.

  • TAM (total addressable market): total demand if everyone who could use the service used it.
  • SAM (serviceable available market): the part you can reach with your offer and channels.
  • SOM (serviceable obtainable market): the share you can realistically win.

5.2 Sizing approaches

Top-down

Start from a larger known total and apply shares.

Market = total market × share relevant to the segment × share of the product

Fast, but depends on the quality of the starting figure.

Bottom-up

Build from units, adoption and price.

Market = number of potential users × adoption rate × annual spend per user

Transparent and testable; requires more data.

Always do both and compare. If they diverge strongly, one of the assumptions is wrong.

Worked example (illustrative assumptions)

Market for lane-level positioning in new cars in the EU in 2034.

  • New cars sold per year: assume 10 million [check the current figure].
  • Share with lane-level GNSS (needed for automated driving): 30% in 2034.
  • Extra hardware value per car: €40; correction service: €20 per car per year.
  • Hardware market 2034 = 10m × 30% × €40 = €120m.
  • Service market depends on the installed base (cars on the road equipped), not on annual sales: if 15 million equipped cars are active in 2034, services = 15m × €20 = €300m. Services end up larger than hardware, consistent with EUSPA's view that services drive GNSS revenues.

5.3 Growth and forecasting

CAGR = (Valueend / Valuestart)1/n − 1
Rule of 72: years to double ≈ 72 / growth rate (%)

Example: €300bn → €580bn in 10 years: (1.933)0.1 − 1 = 6.8%. Check with the rule of 72: at 6.8%, doubling takes about 10.6 years, consistent with almost doubling in 10.

Adoption curves and the Bass model

New technologies spread along an S-curve: slow start, rapid growth, saturation. The Bass diffusion model captures this:

New adopters in period t = [ p + q × N(t−1) / m ] × [ m − N(t−1) ]
m = market potential; N = cumulative adopters; p = innovation coefficient (external influence, e.g. advertising, regulation); q = imitation coefficient (word of mouth)

Typical values in the literature: p around 0.01–0.03, q around 0.3–0.5. Regulation (e.g. eCall making GNSS mandatory) acts like a jump in adoption.

Forecasting practice

  • Drivers: regulation, prices, technology readiness, user awareness, competing solutions.
  • Scenarios: low, base, high, with explicit assumptions.
  • Calibration: check the model against historical data and expert views.
  • Consistency: the installed base must equal past shipments minus retired devices.

5.4 Value chain analysis

Used, for example, in the project on semiconductors for defence and space.

  1. Split the chain into stages (for chips: design → fabrication → assembly and test → integration in equipment → platforms).
  2. Map the players in each stage (EU and non-EU), their capacity and market share.
  3. Identify dependencies and chokepoints: single suppliers, non-EU control, export restrictions (e.g. US ITAR), radiation-hardened components.
  4. Forecast demand: number of platforms (satellites, missiles, aircraft) × components per platform × price, by scenario.
  5. Compare supply and demand, and derive policy options.

In defence and space, volumes are small but critical: the issue is security of supply, not market size.

5.5 Market and technology monitoring

  • Fixed list of indicators per segment (shipments, installed base, revenues, prices, penetration of Galileo-enabled devices).
  • Recurring sources: company reports, industry databases, trade press, patents, start-up funding rounds, standards bodies, regulatory changes.
  • Technology watch: readiness levels (TRL), new entrants, cost trends.
  • Validation with experts; documented methodology so updates are consistent.

5.6 Primary research

Surveys

  • Define the population and the sampling method (random, stratified by segment, or purposive when the population is small and specialised).
  • Short questionnaire; closed questions for indicators, a few open questions; neutral wording; pilot test.
  • Watch for bias: self-selection (only enthusiasts answer), social desirability, leading questions.
Margin of error (95%, proportion p) = 1.96 × √[ p(1 − p) / n ]
For p = 0.5: n = 385 gives ±5%; n = 200 gives ±6.9%; n = 100 gives ±9.8%

In B2B and institutional surveys the population is often small, so results are indicative: combine them with interviews.

Interviews and workshops

  • Semi-structured interviews: a guide of open questions, same core for everyone, freedom to follow up. Surveys tell you what, interviews tell you why.
  • Case studies: in-depth analysis of a few examples to illustrate mechanisms.
  • Workshops and focus groups: validate findings, prioritise options.
  • Delphi method: several anonymous rounds of expert estimates with feedback, until views converge. Useful for forecasts with little data.

When sources disagree

  1. Check definitions and scope (devices vs services; EU vs global; revenue vs value added).
  2. Check year, currency, nominal vs real.
  3. Check method and who produced the figure (and their incentives).
  4. Reconcile, or present a range, and state clearly which figure you use and why.

7. Excel, data and presentation

7.1 Building a model that survives review

  • Separate sheets: Inputs (all assumptions, with source and date) → Calculations → Outputs (results, charts).
  • No hard-coded numbers inside formulas. Every assumption lives in one cell.
  • Consistent timeline across columns; one unit per row, clearly labelled (€m, real 2024 prices).
  • Checks: totals that must match, balance checks, flags for errors.
  • Colour convention: inputs in one colour, formulas in another.
  • Version control: date and version in the file name; change log.

7.3 Writing and slides

  • Pyramid principle: start with the answer, then the supporting arguments, then the evidence.
  • Action titles: each slide title states the conclusion ("GNSS revenues in road will double by 2034, driven by services"), not the topic ("Road market").
  • One message per slide; charts labelled with units and sources.
  • Executive summary readable on its own; methodology in the annex.
  • For non-technical audiences: the result, the three main drivers, what could change it, what is not monetised.

8. AI in consulting work

8.1 Useful applications in this team

  • Desk research: screening many documents, summarising regulation, finding relevant sources faster.
  • Data extraction: pulling figures and tables from long PDFs into structured data.
  • Coding: Python or Excel formulas for data cleaning, analysis and charts.
  • Drafting: first versions of sections, interview guides, survey questions, slide outlines.
  • Qualitative analysis: first coding of interview notes and open survey answers into themes.
  • Market monitoring: scanning news and company announcements to update databases.

8.2 Rules for using it well

  • Verify every fact and number against a primary source: models can invent figures and references.
  • Confidentiality: client data and non-public documents go only into approved enterprise tools, never into public ones.
  • Traceability: keep track of sources so the client can check them.
  • Human judgement: conclusions and recommendations remain the team's responsibility.
  • Measure the value: time saved, quality gained. Consultancies increasingly ask juniors to identify, quantify and track the value created by AI.

8.3 EU AI Act in brief

  • Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, in force since August 2024, with staggered application.
  • Risk-based: prohibited practices; high-risk systems with strict requirements (data quality, documentation, human oversight); transparency obligations (e.g. disclose AI-generated content); minimal-risk systems largely free.
  • Specific obligations for general-purpose AI models.
  • Prohibitions applied first (from February 2025), then obligations for general-purpose models (from August 2025), then most other rules. [check] The Commission has proposed adjustments to the timetable for some high-risk obligations.
"I use AI to go faster on research and first drafts, but every number goes back to a primary source, and client data only go into approved tools. The judgement stays with us."

9.3 Framework for "estimate the market for X"

  1. Clarify the definition (product, geography, year, revenue type).
  2. Choose the driver: users, devices, area (hectares), transactions.
  3. Bottom-up: number × adoption × price; state each assumption and round numbers.
  4. Sanity check with a top-down figure or a known benchmark.
  5. Give a range and the most uncertain assumption.

Mini-case, spoken answer

Q: "How big is the market for satellite-based crop monitoring services for large farms in the EU?"

"Let me define it as annual spending by farms above 50 hectares on satellite-based advisory services. Bottom-up: I'd take the number of such farms from Eurostat — say a few hundred thousand — times an adoption rate, perhaps 10 to 15% today, times an annual subscription of a few hundred to a thousand euros. That gives tens of millions of euros a year. I'd cross-check against EUSPA's market report, where agriculture is the largest Earth Observation segment at around a fifth of €3.5 billion. The most uncertain assumption is adoption, so I'd present a range and test it with interviews of service providers."

9.4 Personal questions: STAR

  • Situation: context in one or two sentences.
  • Task: your responsibility.
  • Action: what you did, step by step (the longest part, in the first person).
  • Result: outcome with numbers, and what you learned.

Prepare at least: a research task you managed end to end; tight deadline or changing priorities; disagreement; defending your analysis; a mistake; learning a new topic fast; using AI concretely. Write them in English in the "Personal questions" tab of the trainer.
